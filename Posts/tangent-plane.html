A Measure-Theoretic Notion of a Tangent Plane

Review of the Classical Notion of a Tangent Plane

Before we move on to our measure-theoretic construction of a tangent plane, we should first review the notion of a tangent plane that we are all familiar with.

First, we introduce the definition of a \( C^1 \) surface: \[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{\underline{Definition.}}\\ M \subset \mathbb{R}^n \text{ is a } k \text{-dimensional } C^1 \text{ surface if it is the image of a } C^1 \text{ bijection }\\ f : E \subset \mathbb{R}^k \rightarrow M\\ \text{ with } Jf \neq 0 \text{ everywhere on } E. \end{gathered}} \] Here \( Jf(x) = \sqrt{\det ( \nabla f(x)^T \nabla f(x))}\) is the Jacobian of \( f \). The requirement that \( Jf \neq 0 \) is just to prevent our surface from having cusps.

With this definition of a \( C^1 \) surface in hand, we can reformulate our notion of a tangent plane as \[ T_{f(x)} M = \left\{ \nabla f(x)y \ | \ y \in \mathbb{R}^k \right\}. \] This definition is motivated by the Taylor expansion for a \(C^1 \) function: \[ f(y) = f(x)(y-x) + \nabla f(x)(y-x) + \mathcal{O}(|y-x|), \] and so the first two terms give us our desired linear approximation to \( f \).

The Measure-Theoretic Definition of a Tangent Plane

Our notion of a tangent plane so far fully relies on our usage of derivatives, which further relies on our having obtained a function to parametrize the surface. But what happens if our surface is extremely complex, and a parametrization is difficult to obtain? Can we reformulate our notion of a tangent plane in other ways?
One such way is through the usage of measure theory.

Take a \( C^1 \) surface \( \Gamma \). For \(x \in \Gamma \), and for a small number \( r > 0 \), form the set \[ \Gamma_{r,x} = \{ y \ | \ ry + x \in \Gamma \}. \] So, \( \Gamma_{r,x} \) consists of all those points that, after being shrunk by \( r \) and shifted to \( x \), lie on \( \Gamma \). In other words, \( \Gamma_{r,x} \) is the surface obtained by zooming in to \( \Gamma \) at the point \( x \) by the factor \( r \), then shifting everything to the origin. Using terminology that will be used later, we are "blowing up" the surface at the point \( x \).

Intuitively, we see that, for very small \( r \), our surface \( \Gamma_{r,x} \) looks similar to the tangent plane \(T_x \Gamma \). As a result, we should expect that \[ \mathcal{H}|_{\Gamma_{r,x}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}|_{T_x \Gamma}. \]

Can we prove that our intuition holds?

A simple case would be a \(C^1\) curve in \( \mathbb{R}^n \), \[ \Gamma = \gamma((a,b)), \ \ \gamma : (a,b) \subset \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n \text{ is } C^1 \text{ and injective with } \gamma' \neq 0 \text{ on } (a,b). \] Then the tangent plane at \( x_0 = \gamma(t_0) \) is \[ T_{x_0} \Gamma = \left\{ s\gamma'(t_0) \ | \ s \in \mathbb{R} \right\}. \] First note that, for any Borel \( A \subset \mathbb{R}^n \), \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}|_{\Gamma_{x_0,r}} (A) &= \mathcal{H} (A \cap \{y \mid ry+x_0 \in \Gamma \})\\ &= \mathcal{H} \left( A \cap \frac{\Gamma-x_0}{r} \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{r} \mathcal{H} \left( rA \cap (\Gamma-x_0) \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{r} \mathcal{H} \left( (rA + x_0) \cap \Gamma \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{r} (\Phi_{x_0,r})_\# \mathcal{H}|_\Gamma (A), \end{aligned} \] where we have defined \( \Phi_{x_0,r}(y) = \frac{y-x_0}{r} \).
So, for any \( \varphi \in C_c^0(\mathbb{R}^n) \), \[ \begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \varphi d \mathcal{H}|_{\Gamma_{x_0,r}} &= \frac{1}{r} \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \varphi d \left( (\Phi_{x_0,r})_\# \mathcal{H}|_\Gamma \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{r} \int_{\Gamma} (\varphi \circ \Phi_{x_0,r}) d \mathcal{H}\\ &= \frac{1}{r} \int_{\Gamma} \varphi \left( \frac{y-x_0}{r} \right) d \mathcal{H}(y)\\ &= \frac{1}{r} \int_a^b \varphi \left( \frac{\gamma(t) - \gamma(t_0)}{r} \right) |\gamma'(t)| dt\\ &= \int_{-\frac{t_0-a}{r}}^{\frac{b-t_0}{r}} \varphi \left( \frac{\gamma(t_0 + rs) - \gamma(t_0)}{r} \right) |\gamma'(t_0 + rs)|ds\\ &\rightarrow \int_{\mathbb{R}} \varphi(s \gamma'(t_0)) |\gamma'(t_0)|ds = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \varphi d\mathcal{H}|_{T_{x_0} \Gamma}, \ \ \text{ as } r \rightarrow 0^+. \end{aligned} \] And so we have proved our claim!

It turns out that, with a few more powerful tools, the above can be extended to any \( k \)-dimensional \( C^1 \) surface \(\Gamma \subset \mathbb{R}^n \), i.e., that \( \mathcal{H}|_{\Gamma_{x_0,r}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}|_{T_{x_0} \Gamma}\).
As a result, it is natural to define a tangent plane to a Borel set \( M \subset \mathbb{R}^n \) at \( x \in M \) as the \( k \)-dimensional surface \( \pi_x \) such that \( \mathcal{H}|_{M_{x,r}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}|_{\pi_x} \), if it exists.
In this case, we call \( \mathcal{H}|_{M_{x,r}} \) the \( k \)-dimensional blow-ups of \( \mathcal{H}|_M \) at \( x \).

Rectifiable Sets

Can we weaken the assumption on our sets, and still obtain tangent planes?
A natural weaker assumption to work with than \( C^1 \) surfaces are Lipschitz surfaces. This way, "rough" behavior along the boundary is allowed, such as cusps and corners. We can further weaken our assumption by allowing the surface to be "Lipschitz almost everywhere." This leads into the definition of a Rectifiable surface.
\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{\underline{Definition.}}\\ M \subset \mathbb{R}^n \text{ is countably } \mathcal{H}^k \text{-rectifiable if there exists countably many Lipschitz maps}\\ f_i : \mathbb{R}^k \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n \text{ such that}\\ \mathcal{H}^k \left( M \backslash \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} f_i(\mathbb{R}^n) \right) = 0.\\ \text{We say that } M \text{ is locally } \mathcal{H}^k \text{-rectifiable if we also have}\\ \mathcal{H}^k(M \cap K) < \infty\\ \text{ for every compact } K \subset \mathbb{R}^n. \end{gathered}} \] Should we intuitively still be able to obtain tangent planes? Rademacher's Theorem gives us hope that our answer is yes:
\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{\underline{Rademacher's Theorem.}}\\ \text{If } f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m \text{ is a Lipschitz function, then } f \text{ is differentiable a.e. on } \mathbb{R}^n. \end{gathered} } \] So, if Lipschitz functions admit tangent planes at almost every point, and Rectifiable sets are covered almost everywhere by Lipschitz functions, we have reason to think that Rectifiable sets admit tangent planes almost everywhere.

Rectifiable Sets Admit Tangent Planes

\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{\underline{Theorem.}}\\ \text{If } M \subset \mathbb{R}^n \text{ is locally } \mathcal{H}^k \text{-rectifiable, then for } \mathcal{H}^k \text{-a.e. } x \in M,\\ \text{ there exists a unique } k \text{-dimensional plane } \pi_x \text{ such that}\\ \mathcal{H}^k|_{M_{x,r}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}^k|_{\pi_x}, \ \ \text{as } r \rightarrow 0^+. \end{gathered} } \] Let's discuss the idea of the proof.
First, we note that a Rectifiable set \( M \) has the property that it can be expressed as a union of countably many Lipschitz maps \( f_i : \mathbb{R}^k \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n \), and bounded Borel sets \(E_i \subset \mathbb{R}^k \), such that \[ M = M_0 \cup \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} f_i(M_i), \ \ \mathcal{H}^k(M_0) = 0. \] Further, the pairs \( (f,E) \) satisfy nice enough parametrization properties ( \(Jf \neq 0, \) \( f \) is injective, etc...)
With this in hand, we then use the property that the measure-theoretic tangent planes agree with the classical tangent planes on each piece of this decomposition, i.e., \[ \mathcal{H}^k|_{(M_i)_{x,r}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}^k|_{\nabla f(x)(\mathbb{R}^k)}, \ \ \text{for each } x \in M_i. \] And so, after arguing that \( M \backslash M_i \) has no contribution to the tangent plane, i.e., \[ \mathcal{H}^k|_{(M \backslash M_i)_{x,r}} \rightharpoonup 0, \ \ \text{for each } x \in M_i, \] we have, \[ \mathcal{H}^k|_{M_{x,r}} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}^k|_{\nabla f_i(x)(\mathbb{R}^k)}, \ \ \text{for all } x \in M \backslash M_0, \text{ and for some } i.\] Since \( \mathcal{H}^k(M_0) = 0 \), the result follows.

The Converse

A natural question to ask is, can our assumption be weakened even further? In other words, do there exist sets that have tangent planes at almost every point, but are not Rectifiable?
It turns out that the answer is no - in fact, being rectifiable is the absolute weakest condition needed for tangent planes to exist almost everywhere. This is shown by the following Theorem: \[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{\underline{Theorem.}}\\ \text{If } \mu \text{ is a Radon measure on } \mathbb{R}^n, M \subset \mathbb{R}^n \text{ is Borel, } \mu \text{ is concentrated on } M,\\ \text{and, for every } x \in M, \text{ there is a } k \text{-dimensional plane } \pi_x \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^n \text{ such that}\\ \mu_{x,r} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}^k|_{\pi_x}, \ \ \text{as } r \rightarrow 0^+,\\ \text{then } \mu = \mathcal{H}^k|_M \text{ and } M \text{ is locally } \mathcal{H}^k \text{-rectifiable.} \end{gathered} } \] Let's discuss the idea of the proof.
Our goal is to show that the set \( M \) can be covered up to a set of measure zero by images of Lipschitz maps.
But recall the geometric interpretation of Lipschitz function: A Lipschitz function is a function such that, at every point we can place the vertex of a cone, and the graph of the function will lie entirely within that cone. Further, the "size of the opening" of the cone remains constant for every point.

So let \( M' \subset M \) be compact. If we had that, uniformly for \( x \in M' \), \[ \lim_{r \rightarrow 0^+} \frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{\mathcal{L}^k(B(x,r))} = 1\] \[ \lim_{r \rightarrow 0^+} \frac{\mu(B(x,r) \backslash (x + K(\pi_x,1)))}{\mathcal{L}^k(B(x,r))} = 0,\] then we should expect \( M' \) to be \( \mathcal{H}^k \)-rectifiable.
(Here, \( K(\pi, t) \) denotes the cone \( K(\pi, t) = \{ y \in \mathbb{R}^n \ | \ |y| < \sqrt{1 + t^2} | p_{\pi} y | \} \), where \( p_{\pi} : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n \) is the orthogonal projection onto \( \pi \). These cones contain \( \pi \) for every \( t > 0 \). They converge to \( \pi \) as \( t \rightarrow 0^+ \), and they converge to \( \mathbb{R}^n \) as \( t \rightarrow \infty \)). Why should we expect rectifiability? The first limit relation essentially says that \( M' \) is \( k \)-dimensional at very small scales, since, for \( r \) very small, \( \mu(B(x,r)) \approx \mathcal{L}^k(B(x,r)) \).
The second limit relation essentially says that the "amount" of \( M' \) lying inside \( B(x,r) \) but outside the cone \( x + K(\pi_x,1) \) is neglegible compared to the total measure of \( B(x,r) \).
Contrasting with our geometric interpretation of Lipschitz functions, \( M' \) behaves "almost" like a Lipschitz function at very small scales.

The bulk of the proof is making this intuition rigorous. Also, it can be shown that these limit relations do indeed hold for compact sets \( M' \subset M \). By applying this result to the compact sets \( M \cap B(0,R) \), we obtain that \( M \) is rectifiable.
